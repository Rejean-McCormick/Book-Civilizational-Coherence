# Founder Fallibility

## Principle

A long-lived system should assume that its founder will eventually be wrong about something consequential.

Founder error is therefore not an exceptional edge case. It is an expected condition of continuity.

\[
Founder \neq System
\]

\[
FounderAssertion \neq CanonicalTruth
\]

## Why this matters

A system that remains healthy only while its founder is correct is still dependent on founder correctness.

Failure can arise through ordinary mechanisms:

- incomplete information;
- changing conditions;
- overconfidence;
- poor judgment;
- value conflict;
- cognitive decline;
- capture or coercion;
- simple human error.

The architecture should not need to predict which one occurs.

## Required capacities

A robust community should be able to:

1. isolate a disputed proposition from the rest of the corpus;
2. inspect evidence and assumptions;
3. oppose the founder without being treated as disloyal to the system;
4. organize counterarguments and affected-party input;
5. use legitimate institutional correction mechanisms;
6. preserve useful parts of the project while rejecting the disputed part;
7. fork where necessary;
8. record the disagreement and outcome as reusable institutional memory.

## Opposition as a health signal

The absence of visible opposition is not automatically evidence of coherence.

In some contexts, the ability to generate organized, legitimate opposition is itself a resilience property.

\[
HealthyCoherence \neq Obedience
\]

## Relation to founder decentering

Founder decentering does not require mandatory founder disappearance or dispossession.

The stronger requirement is that authority, operation, reproduction and interpretation can survive disagreement with the founder and do not depend on unique founder permission.
