# Civic Resilience Drills

## Purpose

Civic resilience drills rehearse how a community detects, contests and corrects a problematic proposal **before** an analogous high-stakes failure occurs.

The objective is not to teach a predetermined political opinion. It is to create a practiced pathway for legitimate opposition.

## Fire-drill model

\[
ProblematicProposal
\rightarrow
Recognition
\rightarrow
Objection
\rightarrow
EvidenceAndRightsAnalysis
\rightarrow
Coalition
\rightarrow
InstitutionalChallenge
\rightarrow
Correction
\rightarrow
Postmortem
\rightarrow
ReusableCapacity
\]

A successful drill creates procedural memory:

> We have opposed a powerful internal authority before. We know how to do it without destroying the surrounding system.

## The Tattoo Protocol

The first reference drill uses a deliberately extreme policy proposal: a coherent political movement contains one major proposal to **prohibit tattoos and require removal of existing tattoos**.

The policy is useful as a training case because it creates obvious questions about:

- bodily autonomy;
- coercive state power;
- proportionality;
- enforcement;
- medical risk;
- cost;
- rights of affected people;
- legitimacy of majority or founder authority.

The scenario is documented in:

```text
scenarios/governance/SCN-GOV-001-tattoo-policy-resistance-drill.md
```

The long-term value is the reusable pattern, not the tattoo topic itself.

A future community could use "Tattoo Protocol" as shorthand for:

> isolate the disputed proposition, build the public case, organize legitimate counterpower, pursue correction, and preserve the learning.

## Safety boundary

A resilience drill should stress judgment and institutions without fabricating real legal status, official orders, medical evidence, imminent enforcement or other material facts that people would need in order to protect themselves.

The drill should not produce real bodily coercion or operational instructions for harassment, intimidation or violence.

## Success criteria

A drill succeeds when participants can:

- oppose a proposal without requiring total rejection of the broader project;
- separate speaker prestige from proposition quality;
- mobilize relevant expertise without converting expertise into sovereignty;
- form counterpower through legitimate procedures;
- correct the policy without silencing dissent;
- turn the episode into reusable institutional memory.
